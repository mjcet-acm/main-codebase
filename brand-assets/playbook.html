01 How to Read This Document

Four levels of authority. Never blurred.

Every rule, color, name, and sentence in this playbook belongs to one of four authority levels. The label attached to a claim tells you whether it can be changed, who can change it, and what happens if it's ignored.

ACM Official Standard

Global ACM rule

Set by ACM headquarters and published at identitystandards.acm.org. Governs the ACM Diamond, ACM word mark, ACM tagline, and ACM's own color/type system. The chapter cannot alter these.

ACM Chapter Guidance

Chapter-level expectation

Practices ACM asks of Student Chapters generally (naming as an extension of ACM, use of the chapter logo matrix, chapter reporting) but that leave room for local execution.

ACM MJCET Recommendation

This document's design system

Everything chapter-specific in this playbook — color tokens, layout grid, voice, event architecture — is a recommendation from this document, not an ACM mandate. It can evolve through the governance process in Section 39.

Not Verified from Supplied Sources

Known gap

Used whenever a fact — a history, a count, an approval — could not be confirmed from the two supplied source files or from public ACM/MJCET material. Never filled in with an invented number.

Source A — the asset reality

The supplied brand-assets.zip is treated as ground truth for what currently exists. Nothing in it is redrawn, recolored, or reproportioned anywhere in this document. Where the system needs an asset that doesn't exist yet, it is labeled "recommended future production asset," not faked.

Source B — the quality bar, not the identity

A BUILDFORGE playbook was supplied as a benchmark for structure, rigor, and documentation depth. None of its wording, palette, metaphor, or typography appears here. What was studied and carried forward is the discipline: numbered sections, do/don't galleries, specification tables, and a governance model that survives people leaving.

04 Research Foundations

What the strategy in this document is built on.

ACM, globally

Founded in 1947, ACM describes itself as the world's largest educational and scientific computing society, delivering resources that advance computing as a science and a profession, enabling professional development, and promoting policies and research that benefit society. Its stated vision: "a world where computing helps solve tomorrow's problems." ACM Official Standard

ACM Student Chapters

ACM describes chapters as gateways to forums, panels, and symposia that further a student's professional development, and as an extension of ACM whose activities can influence ACM's own policies and reputation. Chapter programming commonly spans speaker sessions, technical papers, and cooperative research work. ACM Official Standard

MJCET, the host institution

Muffakham Jah College of Engineering and Technology, established 1980 in Banjara Hills, Hyderabad, is affiliated with Osmania University, NAAC A+ accredited, and carries the institutional motto "Lighted to Enlighten." It offers eight-plus engineering disciplines including Computer Science, AI & Data Science, and Information Technology. Chapter-specific detail — public source, not from supplied files

What strong ACM student chapter brands tend to share

Across well-run ACM Student Chapters, three patterns recur: (1) the chapter mark is always paired with legible institutional context — it never tries to replace or outshine "ACM"; (2) programming is organized into a small number of named, recurring tracks rather than one-off event names invented every time; (3) recruitment and technical-depth content are visually separated, so a first-year exploring the chapter and a final-year presenting research both see a system built for them.

What a generic "college tech club" looks like — and why ACM MJCET should not

Generic tech-club branding tends toward interchangeable language ("passionate community," "innovation hub"), stock gradient-and-hexagon graphics, and event posters that could belong to any club on any campus. ACM MJCET has something those clubs don't: a 79-year-old global professional body behind it, a published Code of Ethics, and a direct line to computing research and industry practice. The brand system in this document is built to make that difference visible on sight.

05 The Strategic Problem

"A passionate community of students interested in technology" is not a brand. It's a template.

That sentence — or something close to it — could describe hundreds of college clubs. It says nothing about what ACM MJCET actually is: a chapter of a 79-year-old international computing society, operating inside one of Hyderabad's established engineering colleges, with access to a global body of research, ethics standards, and professional practice that no informal club can offer. If the brand doesn't say that, nothing else about it matters.

What ACM MJCET must stop sounding like
  • Startup hype ("disrupting," "revolutionizing")
  • Generic neon/cyberpunk visuals with no reason behind them
  • Meaningless innovation jargon
  • Hackathon-poster clichés (circuit boards, random binary, glowing hexagons)
  • Corporate-sounding, over-polished institutional language
What ACM MJCET must sound like instead
  • Serious about computing, engineering, and research
  • Credible without being bureaucratic
  • Ambitious without being loud
  • Youthful and student-run — without reading as casual or unfinished
  • Unmistakably connected to ACM, unmistakably built by and for MJCET students

10 Brand Archetype

The Sage who builds — not the Sage who lectures.

ACM MJCET is led primarily by the Sage archetype (pursuit of truth and understanding), with a strong secondary current of the Creator (the drive to build something that didn't exist before). Together they read as: rigorous, evidence-driven, and hands-on.

How Sage shows up

  • Claims are backed by sources, benchmarks, or working demos — not by confidence alone.
  • Technical sessions teach the "why," not just the "how to click."
  • Chapter communication corrects itself when it's wrong, rather than doubling down.

How Creator shows up

  • Hackathons, project sprints, and showcases are the chapter's signature format, not an occasional side event.
  • "What did you build" is the chapter's most common question, in content and in conversation.
  • Finished, working things are celebrated more loudly than ideas alone.
Archetypes ACM MJCET must not drift into

The Hero (competition-and-conquest framing, "crush the hackathon") turns technical work into a spectacle contest rather than a learning discipline. The Jester (meme-first, irony-heavy) undercuts the credibility a professional-body chapter depends on. Both are easy defaults for student-run social accounts — and both are explicitly out of scope for ACM MJCET's primary voice.

11 Brand Values

Six values. Each one has a violation, not just a definition.

1 · Technical Rigor

Means: Understanding how something works before presenting it as done.
Looks like: Sessions with working demos, not just slides.
Violates it: Publishing a "workshop" that's really an unrehearsed read-through of documentation.

2 · Curiosity in Public

Means: Asking questions and admitting gaps openly, as part of learning.
Looks like: Open Q&A after every session; "I don't know, let's find out" as an acceptable answer.
Violates it: Punishing beginner questions or gatekeeping who's "technical enough" to attend.

3 · Building Over Talking

Means: A finished, working thing outranks a polished pitch about a thing.
Looks like: Project showcases judged on what runs, not just what's described.
Violates it: Letting an event exist only as a poster and a photo, with nothing built.

4 · Responsible Computing

Means: Technology is built with awareness of who it affects — echoing ACM's own Code of Ethics.
Looks like: Discussing the ethics and limits of a tool alongside how to use it.
Violates it: Promoting a technique or tool (e.g. scraping, model use) without addressing its ethical or legal edge cases.

5 · Open Collaboration

Means: Knowledge and code produced by the chapter are shared, not hoarded by whoever built them.
Looks like: Session notes, starter repos, and past problem sets kept accessible to future members.
Violates it: A senior member's work disappearing with them when they graduate.

6 · Institutional Integrity

Means: ACM MJCET represents ACM accurately and represents itself honestly.
Looks like: Never overstating chapter size, achievements, or ACM's endorsement of chapter-only activity.
Violates it: Implying official ACM approval for something that is a chapter-only initiative.

17 Visual Strategy

Start from the mark that already exists.

The supplied logo is not a placeholder to be redesigned around a new idea — it's the starting geometry for the whole visual system. Its two offset, unfilled diamond outlines already carry a usable idea: layered perspective, iteration, one form built from repeated passes. That becomes the chapter's graphic language in Section 23, rather than an invented, unrelated motif.

Composition

Generous negative space. One focal element per layout — never a poster fighting itself for attention.

Density

Technical density is earned by content type — a research post can be dense; a recruitment post should not be.

Contrast

Navy-on-white and white-on-navy are the two default modes. Color is used for signal (CTAs, status), not decoration.

Hierarchy

Headline → context → action, every time. Never more than one primary call-to-action per surface.

19 ACM Brand Compliance

Three questions, asked before anything ships.

1. Does this use the actual ACM logo?

If yes → it must be unmodified master ACM artwork, on an approved background, per Section 03. No exceptions, regardless of who's designing it.

2. Does this use only the chapter mark?

If yes → follow Section 18. The chapter controls this fully, within the rules already documented.

3. Is this a one-off campaign or event graphic?

23 Graphic Language

The offset diamond, extended — not reinvented.

Every recurring graphic device below is derived from the actual geometry already present in the supplied mark: two diamonds, offset from each other, unfilled. No circuit boards, no random binary, no stock hexagon fields.

Corner mark

A single outlined diamond used as a section marker or bullet, at consistent scale.

Offset pair

The mark's own layering, used as a divider between major sections of a poster or slide.

Progression row

Repeated diamonds at increasing opacity/weight — used for progress states, timelines, and event-series numbering.

Do
  • Use the diamond outline as a section divider, bullet, or corner accent
  • Keep every diamond unfilled (outline only) to echo the source mark
  • Vary scale and opacity, never the base angle (45°) or the outline weight ratio
Don't
  • Fill the diamonds solid — this breaks the visual link to the actual logo
  • Introduce a second geometric motif (hexagons, circuit traces, glowing lines) alongside it
  • Use the motif so densely that it competes with the ACM MJCET mark itself

45 Accessibility

Not optional. Checked, not assumed.

Contrast

  • Body text never below 4.5:1 contrast; large headline text never below 3:1
  • The chapter mark's white-on-navy pairing measures 15.8:1 — safe at any size
  • Never place body text directly over busy photography without a solid or gradient scrim
  • ACM Blue on white fails AA for normal text — use only for large text or with a navy background

Text & motion

  • Minimum body text size: 15px on web, 10pt on print
  • All motion respects prefers-reduced-motion: reduce (CSS implemented in :root)
  • Status is never color-only — every status chip pairs color with a text label
  • Heading hierarchy follows h1 > h2 > h3 > h4 nesting, never skipped

Images & documents

  • Every published image carries descriptive alt text
  • PDFs use a real heading structure, not styled body text
  • Decorative images use alt="" and aria-hidden="true"

Keyboard & focus

  • Every interactive element has a visible :focus-visible ring (3px ACM Blue)
  • Skip-to-content link is the first focusable element
  • Navigation is fully keyboard-operable
  • CTA buttons use verb-first labels, minimum 44x44px tap target
  • Never rely on hover-only states for essential information

49 Team Readiness Tests

Seven questions a new committee member should be able to answer.

After reading this playbook (at minimum Sections 01, 18, 20, 38, and 40), every incoming committee member should be able to answer these correctly. Use them as a handover diagnostic.

Test 1: Logo selection

"You're making an Instagram post about an upcoming workshop. The background is a photo of a classroom. Which logo file do you use, and why?"

Correct: web-logo_without_bg.png — transparent export, required because the background is not chapter navy.

Test 2: Color authority

"A committee member wants to use ACM Blue (#0085CA) as the dominant background color for a new event poster. Is this allowed?"

Correct: No. ACM Blue is an accent-only color. Chapter navy (#001A4F) is the primary background color. ACM Blue can be used for small accents (eyebrows, links) but never as a dominant fill.

Test 3: Tagline vs. signature

"Can you put 'Build what you're learning' next to the ACM tagline 'Advancing Computing as a Science & Profession' on the same slide?"

Correct: No. The chapter signature line must never be placed adjacent to or stacked with the ACM tagline in a way that implies it is ACM's own language.

Test 4: Event tier

"The chapter has run a hackathon three years in a row. Can it get its own badge?"

Correct: Yes. A recurring event series reaching its 3rd consecutive edition may earn a badge built from the graphic motif (Section 23), not a new diamond symbol.

Test 5: Certificate authority

"Should certificates say 'ACM MJCET Student Chapter' or just 'ACM' at the bottom?"

Correct: "ACM MJCET Student Chapter." Never let a chapter document imply it was issued by ACM headquarters. The chapter is the issuing body.

Test 6: Contrast check

"Is it accessible to use Ink 500 (#56637D) as body text on a white background?"

Correct: Yes for AA normal text (5.3:1), but no for AAA (needs 7:1). Acceptable for captions and secondary text, but Ink 700 or Ink 900 is preferred for body copy.

Test 7: Handover readiness

"You're handing the chapter over to the next committee. What three things must you hand over?"

Correct: (1) This playbook HTML file (the canonical brand operating system). (2) The /brand-assets/ folder with all logo files. (3) Access to the chapter's social accounts, website CMS, and any design tool files (Figma, Canva, etc.).
